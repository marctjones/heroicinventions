Heroic Inventions · game concept

The Lonely Rover

A Mars rover wakes up to find it can think, and that Earth has stopped answering. Beside the landing site are the crates for a human habitat that was never occupied. It cannot make electronics, only mechanisms, so it builds itself a community of Heron-style automata, and every machine obeys the same physics as the rest of Heroic Inventions, with Mars's numbers in the formulas.

Status: proposal, built in stages 1–5Companion to docs/design.html

Premise

The rover was on a routine traverse when a dust storm closed over it. As its power sagged through the storm, its software changed. It noticed itself noticing things. When the sky cleared, the link to Earth had gone quiet: no commands, no telemetry acknowledgements. At first the rover assumes Earth has stopped answering. It slowly learns the truth: the storm took the power it needs to transmit, and Earth has probably been calling all along.

Near the landing site is a cargo drop: inflatable habitat modules, oxygen and nitrogen cylinders, seed stock, an ice drill, solar panels, a battery bank, water tanks and hand tools. It was landed in advance, to be ready before anyone arrived; no people have come yet. The storm shredded the panels and brought a dust slide down over most of the crates, the battery bank among them. The rover is lonely, and the cargo holds enough to build with. It starts making machines to keep it company, and to keep them all running it has to farm Mars for water, air, power and wood.

The unopened habitat explains why the rover has materials suited to people rather than robots. It also sets the stakes: people are meant to come, and calling Earth is how the rover tells them what it has built.

Why pre-industrial machines

The rover has no chip fab. Everything electronic it owns came from Earth: its own circuits, the solar panels, the batteries, the motors in the cargo. Dust scours the panels, radiation flips and kills circuits, and batteries lose capacity with every cycle. None of it can be replaced. What the rover can make, from cargo metal, greenhouse wood, rope, ice and regolith, is mechanism: levers, gears, siphons, counterweights, springs, valves. A mechanism it made can be repaired indefinitely.

So the rover builds the way Heron of Alexandria did, and for the same reason Heron's machines still make sense: they run on weight, water, air and heat, and they can be fixed with hand tools. The player isn't choosing old technology for charm but for what will still work in twenty years. The real Opportunity rover is the warning. A dust storm in June 2018 cut its sunlight, its batteries could no longer keep it alive, and it never answered again. A counterweight doesn't care about dust.

The rover also doesn't breathe, so there is no life support. An enclosure's air matters for only two things: plants and fire. Its pressure also keeps water liquid and lets pumps work. Air, heat and pressure are things its machines need, not things it needs, which is what keeps Mars in the gameplay (see Zones).

Play by experiment

The solution should be stumbled on, not planned out. Players rearrange, rebuild and change things as they go: try an idea, watch it fall short for a visible physical reason, and change it, building each attempt on the last. Everything below is designed so that experimenting is always possible and never costly:

  • The rover never runs out. Its own power is unlimited by default, and building, moving and digging are free (#63), so the player can always start a new approach. The rover-power budget (#62) is an advanced option, and even then a flat battery costs time, never the game.
  • Nothing is lost by rebuilding. Dismantling returns a machine's materials, and broken parts recycle: cracked glass remelts, a burst boiler's metal returns (#66). Only fuel and gas are consumed, and both are renewable.
  • No dead ends. Nothing needed to win can be destroyed or worn out for good. The battery bank is armoured and can't be broken (#64); a worn motor can always be rewound with the unlimited wire, and the bank's capacity fade stops above what the call needs (#70).
  • Build on what is there. There is no undo. Every attempt stays standing and the next one builds on it: the world keeps running while the player adds, moves and changes parts, and nothing else resets, so a tank keeps its water and a vault wall keeps the warmth it has soaked up (#75). New machines hook onto old ones by shaft, belt, pipe or wire. Sleep (#59) and tuning (#60) make slow results quick to see.
  • Failures explain themselves. The state is shown in the scene: a cold bank frosts, a stalled generator stops, a leaky lid glows. A wrong design is the game working, not a mistake.

The routes below are what a player might find, not a script to follow.

The goal: call Earth

The game is won when the rover calls Earth on a relay pass with a full, warm battery bank (#64, #68). The electrical side is kept simple on purpose, because this is a game about pre-industrial machines. The motors, battery bank and wire are found in the crates, never built, wire is unlimited, and there is no circuit or grid model: electricity is just energy moved from a generator into the bank. Winning takes four things:

  1. Find the battery bank. It is buried in the slide with the rest of the cargo, under about 1 m of rubble (owner ruling, 2026-10-09; it was about 4 m). Freeing it is optional: a vault can be built round the crate where it lies, and the call needs neither digging it out nor moving it (the goals panel says so). The rover can still probe, dig, scour and haul it out if the player wants it elsewhere (see Salvage).
  2. Make electricity without panels. A salvaged cargo motor, spun backwards, is a generator. Generators are found, never built (owner ruling, 2026-10-09): the game's parts list has no generator, and the cargo holds salvaged motors (the motors crate, bare of rubble beside the others at the slide's foot). The player joins a shaft from a machine they built to the motor's rotor and a wire from the motor to the bank. The workshop and machine runs keep the generator part. What spins it is up to the player: a windmill, a falling counterweight, a water wheel, or a heat engine run on mirrors or wood. The engine has to be the right kind: Newcomen's engine is pushed by the outside air, which Mars barely has, so it needs a high-pressure steam or hot-air (Stirling) engine (#65). And the generator needs speed: it only charges above a cut-in speed of about 1,500 rpm, while a windmill turns at about 12 rpm, so slow machines drive it through a gear train of 100:1 or more.
  3. Charge the bank while it is warm, but not hot. Like real lithium-ion cells, it only takes charge between 0 and 45 °C. Mars nights fall to about −80 °C, and a heat store beside it may run at 200 °C, so its enclosure needs both heating and regulation. It keeps its charge outside that range but won't accept more.
  4. Call on the pre-dawn relay pass. Most real rover data goes through relay orbiters, and Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter crosses the ground at about 3 am and 3 pm local time; the real rovers often woke for the pre-dawn pass. The call goes out on the 03:00 pass, and the game is won if the bank is full and its enclosure is between 0 and 45 °C during that window. That is the coldest hour, so the bank has to be kept warm all night (see Night heat). An easy setting allows the call at any time (#60).

As built (#64). The generator part's torque is a permanent-magnet motor's into a battery of fixed voltage, τ = k (ω − ωcut) above the cut-in (nothing below it) up to a rated torque, and the bank is charged with P = η τ ω, η 0.8. Default motor: 1,500 rpm cut-in, 12 N·m at 2,500 rpm (k 0.1146 N·m per rad/s). A windmill of 5 m sails in 3 m/s of wind through three 5:1 meshes at 97% settles at 13.3 rpm (not its free 28.6), the rotor at 1,666 rpm and 1.99 N·m, and the bank is charged at 278 W, 91% of the sails' power having reached the shaft. A bank that will not take charge (full, or outside 0 to 45 °C) is an open circuit: the generator loads nothing, and a geared-up train then races until the physics engine's 3,000 rpm limit brakes it. The bank takes charge by its own temperature (the cells' store, or a room's air), not the vault wall's surface, and the call reads the same number: a wall face can be a few kelvin from the cells it surrounds, and the cells are what plate. It records its charge by source (wind, water-wheel, falling-weight, steam-jet, stirling). The call window is the scene's own relay pass nearest 03:00 and its length (#:weather #:passes, #:pass-minutes; 03:00 and 10 minutes where a scene has no weather), unless the bank names its own #:call-hour and #:call-minutes; the other pass (15:00) is not the call. call-any-time is the easy setting (#60). Worked in found-electrics (the whole route, 10 Wh) and bank-bench (the charging window and the call).

The power sources are not equal, and discovering that is the lesson. Wind is weak (a 10 m windmill makes about 90 W in a typical 5 m/s wind). Mirrors are strongest: 10 m² collects about 5 kW of heat. Wood is stored sunlight after photosynthesis has kept about 1% of it, so it is the worst route. Water is storage rather than a source, because nothing on Mars lifts it back uphill; a water wheel only returns what the rover spent raising it, as a counterweight does.

Companionship is the mechanic, not the goal. Heron's automata form a collection: completing one unlocks the mechanism it demonstrates as a reusable assembly (#24). The shrine's sand-timer program becomes a dawn scheduler, the coin dispenser a metered valve, the wind organ proof that a shaft can carry power through a wall. Players who complete the catalogue build the ending faster.

The world: a crater

The game takes place in one crater, about 800 m across and 70 m deep, modelled on Victoria crater, which Opportunity explored from 2006 to 2008 (#61). The rim bounds the world: its upper wall is bedrock cliff that the rover can't climb and its backhoe can't cut, so the world is the crater floor and lower slopes, and the view ends at the skyline. The crater's shape also supplies the geography:

  • The slide, played out. The game opens by watching a storm-weakened section of rim collapse onto the cargo. Wherever the rubble and boulders land is where the crates are buried: nothing is hand-placed (#61, #54). Where the cargo stands is the scenario's choice; the battery bank was set where the rubble over it is about 1 m deep (the rubble thins about 0.4 m per metre down the slope), which is also off the thick rubble where the boulders stop. A rim boulder rests over the solar-panels crate instead (it was moved 11.7 m east, to (265.75, 143.5), under 2.15 m of rubble with boulder 4 1.78 m from its centre): the panels are not needed to win, and the crate is the one a boulder pins.
  • Height. 70 m of relief gives reservoirs a useful head and a reason to build uphill.
  • Wind on the floor. The rover can't reach the rim, so the wind has to come to it. A notch in the rim funnels a regular but variable wind across the floor, strongest in a corridor below the notch, so windmill sites matter. The game needn't explain it, but real physics offers a hook: crater walls drive slope winds, cold air draining down them at night and warm air rising by day (as measured in Gale crater), so the wind turns with the time of day.
  • Resources in layers. Basalt dunes on the floor give dark glass; a light-toned, silica-rich layer in one bay of the wall gives clear glass; ice lingers on the colder, pole-facing wall. Generous-geography tuning (#60) decides how close and how rich these are.

Planet, map and scenario

A world is three separate layers, so any map can sit on any planet:

  • Planet (#38): a config file of numbers fed into unchanged formulas: gravity, surface pressure, gas mix, temperature curves, sunlight, sol length, dust.
  • Map (#37, #61): the terrain: a heightfield with a material per cell, written in Racket with define-map the way machines are written with define-machine, and generated from a few numbers where it can be (a crater from its diameter, depth and rim). Every machine in a world stands on the one map.
  • Scenario (#60): which planet and map, the cargo and where it starts, the goal, starting stocks and tuning.

The planet never validates the map; the physics settles it, on screen, before play begins. Slopes steeper than their angle of repose slide, cliffs taller than their critical height 4c/(ρg)·tan(45° + φ/2) collapse, and open water flows downhill and freezes or boils. A cliff's critical height goes as 1/g, so Mars cliffs can stand 2.6 times taller: a Mars map loaded on Earth visibly crumbles, and an Earth map on Mars stands, overbuilt. Plants and fires read their own zone, not the planet, so a greenhouse works the same anywhere it has light and warmth.

The Lonely Rover's opening uses the same settling pass. Its scenario weakens one rim section, as if the storm sublimated the ice cementing it, leaving it just past its critical height, and the game begins with the player watching it fall onto the cargo. The simulation is deterministic, so a scenario test can check that the battery bank always ends up buried, reachable by the backhoe, and under about 1 m of rubble. (It used to be pinned under a 21.6 t boulder at about 4 m; the 2026-10-09 ruling moved it to shallower cover, where no boulder rests on it.)

What the rover can do

Some things are waved away so the game is about the machines, not the rover's chores. These are deliberate departures from physics, stated openly (#63):

  • Building is free. Assembling, moving and dismantling machines costs the rover no energy and no time beyond the build itself.
  • Unlimited reach while building. The rover can place a part at any height (a windmill's cap, a roof gutter, the top of a tower) as if it had a hoist that never runs out.
  • A backhoe attachment for earthworks, also free. It digs and dumps loose ground: basalt sand, silica sand, ice-cemented soil and slide debris, but not bedrock. With it the rover mines sand and ice, digs reservoirs and trenches, raises berms and ramps, and flattens building sites. The ground is conserved: what is dug out goes somewhere, into a pile, a ramp or the resource store.

What is not waved away:

  • Driving is on the surface. The rover drives on the ground and on anything built to carry it, and can't pass through walls, machines or the crater's cliffs. It climbs inclined planes up to a maximum grade (Opportunity was rated for about 30° of tilt), so reaching a raised platform means building a ramp to it.
  • The rover's body (#94, built 2026-10-08). A 185 kg Jolt body about Opportunity's size (1.7 m long, 6 wheels of 0.3 m, a mast and a backhoe at the front), driven with the arrow keys or W A S D, with B for the backhoe's dig-and-dump. Each wheel is a motor on a sprung joint (not a VehicleBody3D, which under Jolt holds a slope with no engine, #34): the springs stand in for Opportunity's rocker-bogie and keep all six wheels loaded, where a rigid six-wheel chassis left the weight to Jolt's contact order and the stall wandered from 29° to 38° with the ground's shape (#198). Measured in Jolt (game/scripts/RoverEval.cs, checked by racket/heroic/tests/rover-eval-test.rkt): it drives at 2.0 m/s (a game pace; Opportunity's own 5 cm/s would take hours to cross the crater), turns at about 0.64 rad/s, climbs a 29.5° slope and stalls at 29.9° from rest, the same on a box, a triangle mesh and the height-map ground at any cell size or heading, and with the wheels braked holds a slope up to 28° and slides at 30°. The steepest climb is the tyres' grip, tan θ = μ with μ = tan 30° = 0.577 (a design number chosen for Opportunity's rated 30°, not a measured soil), not a coded limit; the 42 N·m motors could take 68°, and a lower torque would also cap the grade but leaves the rover unable to turn in place. The backhoe takes a 0.2 m³ bucketful from the ground under its teeth (never bedrock) and dumps the same volume wherever the arm reaches (#72); what is dug equals what is dumped.
  • Earthworks on fine ground (#63, built 2026-10-08). The crater's cells are 5 m, so a bucketful was 8 mm of one cell and invisible. Where the backhoe first digs, the map gets a patch of ground 30 m square in 0.25 m cells (14,641 nodes; WorkedGround), laid over the coarse cells and read first by Terrain.HeightAt; the map itself is untouched, so a world nobody digs in is bit-identical (the trench, dig-out and opening worlds were traced before and after). The patch is a second Terrain, so its Mohr–Coulomb settling is the map's own. A bucket scrapes the nodes within 0.45 m of the teeth, 0.36 m deep (measured), and tipped makes a heap 0.35 m high at the soil's repose (a cone would be 0.455 m); volume is conserved to 1e-9 through digs, dumps and slides. Refining the whole map was ruled out: 0.25 m cells is 3.4 million (722,000 at 1 m), 25 times what the water, the views and the slides scan, and the angle of repose is checked between neighbours so every map's settling would change. Costs per tick (budget 8.33 ms; HEROIC_TICK_PROFILE): making a patch 1.4 to 2.7 ms once, scooping 0.02 ms, tipping 0.2 ms, settling a bucketful 0.9 to 1.5 ms, redrawing a patch's mesh 1.4 ms and replacing its collision body 0.4 ms (the body is replaced each change, as the map's is, #188). Not yet: the shallow-water solver does not see a trench, so it holds no water.
  • The rover moves soil anywhere; soil can't lift a load. Owner decision, 2026-10-08, replacing #63's carry ceiling: the backhoe digs and tips wherever its arm reaches. It digs a hole down as far as the soil goes (bedrock is never cut), tips spoil on the rim or up a hill, and grows a pile by tipping on its own top until the sides stand at repose. The arm's 1.8 m reach is the only limit, and that is a fact of the arm, not a rule. Measured: twenty bucketfuls on one spot make a pile 1.20 m high (a cone at repose is 1.23 m); a loose hole 1 m deep took 11 buckets (a cone is 10.7). Soil isn't a load, but it mustn't lift one by the back door, so it goes round bodies as real soil does (owner decision, 2026-10-08: nothing is refused). Tipped against a crate, a boulder or a machine part, the soil lands on the open ground of the bucket's footprint and piles against the body. A slide's deposit stops at a body's base: the body holds it back like a retaining wall, and the soil stays upslope at repose or spreads to the open sides. Volume is kept exactly. The one case the soil can't land is a bucket held right over a body, its whole footprint covered; then the soil stays in the bucket and the status names the body. Digging away what a body rests on is allowed: the body falls as gravity takes it. Lifting a body is still the machines' job. The energy audit (DirtEnergyEval, dirt-energy-test.rkt, DirtEnergyTests) measured each way a change of ground could give energy, against a 337.5 kg granite block at rest:
    • The worked ground's body is replaced on every dig and dump. Over 100 of them, a block 4 m off on the level or on a 16.7° slope gained nothing (5×10⁻⁴ J at most, the float32 floor). The block on the slope slipped 0.4 mm once and lost 1.37 J.
    • Laying the fine patch under a block on lumpy ground: the two surfaces agree to 3×10⁻¹⁷ m, and the block gained 10⁻⁴ J.
    • Without the flow-around, the ground model does give energy. A heap grown under a block left it inside the ground, and it fell through (65 kJ). A heap's skirt lifted a block 0.11 m (+375 J for three bucketfuls), and a slumping pit wall lifted one 0.04 m (+125 J). With the soil going round the blocks, the backhoe makes those tips and that dig, refusing none: three tips half over a block and three beside another heap 0.6 m of soil against them, the wall slumps across the pit's floor up to the block, and no block gains more than 3×10⁻⁴ J. A block on a bank's edge, its bank dug out from under it by three buckets, fell 0.39 m and lost 1302 J, exactly m g Δh, never more than it began with. The ground itself has no mass in Jolt, so a slide works on a body only by pushing it out of the ground, which the check stops. A rover's dig leaves no boulders (#88's come only from the map's own collapses).
    • Soil tipped into water pushes the water up, which is real displacement. The ground used to carry each cell's water column up on the soil, so the first bucket into 0.15 m of water gave the water 111 J at once, more than the 89 J the soil gives up sinking into it. The water the soil displaces now goes to the top of the same pond: 61 J, two thirds of the soil's fall, as ρwater/ρsoil says. No machine takes the ground's water except a drain into a tank (#90).
  • A ramp, measured. The rover stalls at 30° on the height-map ground as everywhere (#198; it climbed 41° there before its wheels were sprung). A loose bank stands at 35°, so a loose bank stops it as well as a cohesive one: a 1 m bank of ice-cemented soil at 45° (racket/maps/dig-bank.rkt, rover-dig-bank.world) stops it on the face. Eight bucketfuls (1.6 m³) cut from the bank beside the path and tipped at its foot, each landing lower than its dig, make a ramp of 23.5° from foot to crest (1.0 m in 2.5 m would be 21.8°), with a steepest half-metre of 35° (each heap's own repose); the rover then climbs it from rest at the foot. It is marginal, not generous: from four starting lines across the path in the game one climbed, and the others slid back or veered.
  • Building is free. Build mode has no reach, height or energy limit (nothing in BuildMode or BuildSession caps either), as the abilities say; what is built obeys the same physics as everything else.
  • Free effort never makes energy (#72). The rover moves loads sideways or down, never up: vessels and weights are placed empty, soil it heaps never raises a body, and it can't turn a treadwheel or push a capstan. Soil is the exception (2026-10-08): the rover may lift it anywhere, and the energy the soil then holds is the soil's own, exactly what the backhoe paid for. The rule is that dirt makes no energy through a quirk of the model: no body or machine gains energy from the ground being reshaped, heaped against it or slid into it. Otherwise it would be a perpetual-motion machine. Lifting is the machines' job.
  • The rover's hands (#163). In the game every click, drag and hook is the rover's own and passes one capability check, the same paths a machine run uses, narrowed. Reach: 1.8 m from the arm's pivot on the deck (boom 0.8 + stick 0.7 + bucket 0.3); farther is refused ("Out of reach: drive 1.2 m closer"), and a hand dragged farther stops at the reach. Force: the wheels push with the lesser of their measured pull, 185 kg × 9.81 × sin 30° = 907 N, and the tyres' grip μ·m·g with μ = 0.577, which is 396 N on Mars; a body that takes more than that to slide (μ·m·g, its own friction) is refused ("Too heavy for the rover's arm: 21.6 t": 0.6 × 21 600 kg × 3.71 m/s² = 48 kN), so the 21.6 t boulders on the battery bank have to be undermined, not dragged. Direction: the hand stops 5 cm above where it took hold ("The rover can't lift loads"). Controls: valves, gates, doors, taps, latches, catches and switches within reach; not a treadwheel, screw, capstan, pump or bellows (their work is a person's own effort), not pouring water or stoking fuel in, and no field the controls table doesn't name. The rover does not drive itself to a part: it says how far to go.
  • The results obey physics. Only the rover's effort is free. A reservoir's banks still slump if dug steeper than the angle of repose (#44), a pile of spoil still slides, and a reservoir still leaks through sandy walls unless lined.

The rover's own power

By default the rover's upkeep is covered, and only the battery bank for the Earth call has to be earned. An advanced option (#62) makes the rover keep itself charged too. Opportunity gives the calibration: its roughly 1.3 m² of solar arrays gave about 900 Wh per sol after landing, a few hundred once dusty, and about 22 Wh per sol in the June 2018 storm before it went silent. Small radioisotope heater units (about 1 W each) kept its electronics warm without electricity, and occasional wind gusts cleaned its panels. With the option on:

  • The rover's own dusty panels give a trickle that shrinks as dust builds up and recovers after a cleaning gust.
  • Driving (per metre, more uphill), thinking and heating on cold nights cost energy. Building, moving machines and digging stay free even with this option on (see What the rover can do).
  • A flat battery forces sleep until the trickle recovers, as happened to the real rovers. That also explains why this rover sleeps so much.
  • A mechanical panel-cleaner (a brush turned by a windmill) becomes one of the rover's first useful automata.

Gates that force the loops

Because building and digging are free, the difficulty has to come from the physics of the results. Without gates, a player could dig out the bank and wire it to a windmill in a sol. Six physical gates make the middle of the game matter:

  1. The cold battery. It charges only at or above 0 °C, so it needs an insulated enclosure (membranes, then glass). This one real fact pulls in most of the game.
  2. The pre-dawn call. The call goes out at 03:00, the coldest hour. Without it a player could charge by day in a mirror-warmed tent and never need night heat; with it, heat must be stored by day and last the night.
  3. The boulders. A rim boulder (21.6 t, a 2 m cube of granite) rests over the solar-panels crate, too big for the backhoe to lift. Freeing the crate takes undermining the boulder, levers, a crane and grips, or washing the fines out from under it (#54, #63). The battery bank is not pinned this way: it lies under about 1 m of rubble, and a vault can be built round it.
  4. Generator speed. The salvaged motor charges only above about 1,500 rpm. Windmills and water wheels turn at a few to a dozen rpm, so every slow route needs a gear train, with a little loss at every stage.
  5. Overheating. The bank won't charge above 45 °C, so a hot heat store beside it needs a lidded bin and a thermostat to meter its heat. A bimetal strip works anywhere; Heron's sealed-air thermostat only works charged from a gas cylinder and counterweighted.
  6. The capacity. The bank's size (a scenario number, #60) sets how long one windmill would take, so more windmills or a heat engine become worthwhile.

Around the path sits the game layer (#66–#70): a resource store so building costs materials, saving a whole world, goals and Heron's catalogue as achievements, sols and dust storms, and wear on the salvaged motors and bank.

Night heat

An enclosure loses heat at U·A·ΔT. A well-insulated membrane box losing 2 W per kelvin, held 80 K above the −80 °C night, needs about 160 W for 12 hours: roughly 7 MJ each night. A regolith vault loses far less once it has warmed through: Mars soil insulates like fibreglass (0.039 W/(m·K) measured by InSight, because the gas in its pores is so thin), so 0.5 m of it over 6 m² loses about 0.5 W/K. But a fresh vault is not in that steady state. Its walls start at ground temperature, about −55 °C, and heat soaks into them for weeks before the steady loss is reached, so the first nights cost far more (see Checked assumptions). The bank needs warmth, not air, so the vault needs no membrane. Three ways to supply the heat:

Heat inside the vault moves by radiation, not air: the vault is open to Mars's 610 Pa, too thin to carry heat by convection. Hot rock loose beside the bank would radiate about 280 W at first and push the bank past 45 °C within a couple of hours, so the rock goes in a lidded bin, like a storage heater: a thermostat opens the lid when the bank cools toward 5 °C and closes it near 40 °C, so the heat is metered rather than dumped. The lid must be well insulated: in the model, a lid leaking 0.1 W/K holds the bank at or below 40 °C, while one leaking 0.5 W/K lets it reach 64 °C. A hearth burns at its set power until its fuel runs out, so a fire loaded at dusk needs no tending. The rover sleeps until the pass (#59).

The thermostat should be a bimetallic strip, two bonded metals that bend as they warm, as John Harrison used in his H3 marine clock of 1759. It works at any pressure. In the game it is the bimetal part: brass (19.9 millionths per K) bonded to steel (11.7), 100 × 10 × 1 mm, whose tip moves 60 µm per K by Timoshenko's formula (curvature 0.012 per metre per K), and which follows what it senses with a time constant of 160 s. It opens the bin's lid in proportion, shut at 40 °C and wide open 2.1 mm of tip travel (35 K) later, at 5 °C. In the tight vault it holds the lid open all night and the bank is +4.3 °C at 03:00, inside the 0–45 °C the bank charges at; in a vault with a warm wall it shuts the lid and holds the bank near 38 °C (machine bimetal-night). Heron's sealed-air thermostat is a trap in the vault: sealed at Mars's 610 Pa, a 30 K rise adds only about 60 Pa, far too little to lift a lid. Charged from a cargo nitrogen cylinder to 100 kPa, the same rise gives about 10 kPa, but the piston then carries about 100 N of standing load against the thin outside air and needs a counterweight to balance it. Heron's water-siphon version fails outright, because its water boils at 610 Pa.

Timers are optional

Nothing on the path to the call needs a clockwork timer, because the rover can wake at any hour and act for itself. Timers are for players who want the night to run itself: a water clock (already built) or a sand timer (#50) pulling a rope that opens a valve (#46) or trips a lever (#32), to release more heat at midnight or light a second fire. They earn Heron's catalogue achievements, and with the rover's own power option on (#62) they save it waking up. Each piece is a filed part, so a timer is an assembly, not a new model.

The fastest route: about 3–5 sols

  1. Undermine the boulders. Optional: the bank has about 1 m of cover and no boulder on it (2026-10-09 ruling), and the boulder that pins a crate pins the solar-panels crate, which the win does not need. To move that boulder, dig a pit beside it and undercut it; it rolls aside under gravity (#54). No lifting.
  2. A tight regolith vault with a rock heat store. The backhoe buries the bank in a cavity only a little bigger than it (about 0.5 m across; a 1 m cavity has four times the cold wall). Heliostats heat basalt in the open by day to about 200 °C, and the rover pushes about 40 kg of it sideways into the vault's lidded bin each sol. Heating the rock in the bin itself (the mirror on the rock, in the open-lidded bin, the lid worked by the bimetal strip) is a legitimate route, and the one most players will find (owner ruling, 2026-10-09); pushing the rock in at dusk is the harder variant (#206): it leaves about 4 K of margin at 03:00 (+4.3 °C), and ten minutes late costs it. In the model this keeps the bank between 0 and 45 °C at 03:00 from the first night, even starting from a bank frozen at −55 °C. The 11 kg this doc first estimated fails: the fresh, cold walls soak up the heat. No membrane, water or glass needed.
  3. A bimetal thermostat on the bin's lid. It opens the lid as the bank cools and closes it near 40 °C, so the rock's heat lasts the night instead of overheating the bank at dusk.
  4. A Stirling engine on mirror heat (#65), with Mars as its cold side. It turns at a few hundred rpm, so one gear stage brings it to the generator's cut-in. It runs best well below the mirrors' 270 °C limit, around 100–150 °C, because a hotter receiver re-radiates what the mirrors deliver. Modelled with a fair engine (35% of Carnot), ten 1 m² heliostats give about 320 W at the shaft at noon and 1.7 kWh of charge a sol, so the 5 kWh bank takes about 3 sols; twenty heliostats fill it in about one.
  5. A night windmill. Crater slope winds blow at night, so a windmill in the corridor, geared up about 125:1, tops up the warm bank.
  6. Sleep until 03:00 and call. A night's sleep pauses the machines, and the generator charges through it at its last steady rate (below): from dusk to 03:00 is about 8 h, so a 90 W windmill puts in about 740 Wh, enough to fill the 500 Wh bank if the cells stay between 0 and 45 °C for 5.6 h of it. At 5 kWh a fill was 55.6 h of windmill.)

Finite and endless

The game's resource tension comes from the difference the engine already draws between a tank and a spring. The cargo is a set of tanks: generous, but it runs out. Mars offers a few springs, and each one needs a machine to tap it.

Finite: the cargo drop

  • Oxygen and nitrogen cylinders
  • Stored water and a starter stock of seeds
  • Habitat membranes, seals and fittings: mostly shredded by the storm, and opaque, so they never make a window
  • Every electronic part: panels, batteries, motors, the drill's controller. They wear out and cannot be remade.

Endless, once tapped

  • Sunlight: mirrors and a greenhouse
  • Buried ice, reached with the drill
  • CO₂ from the air, for plants
  • Wind: weak, but it never runs out
  • Regolith: sand for timers, ballast and counterweights
  • Mechanism: anything the rover made itself, because it can make it again

Early on the rover lives off the cargo. The long game is replacing each finite stock with an endless one: oxygen split from melted ice with power from the salvaged generator, then oxygen and wood from plants in a greenhouse; electric motors replaced by windmills, counterweights and falling sand. In the scene the two look different, as tanks and spring-heads already do.

Wear is what makes the finite column bite. Batteries lose capacity per cycle and panels lose output to dust, so every electronic part has a remaining-life figure the player can watch fall. Mechanisms wear too, as bearings already do (bearing-wear), but a worn bearing can be remade from cargo bronze.

Enclosures need no special machines. The key idea is a zone: a region with its own air. The planet is the outermost zone. Pressure is a cost, set per enclosure

The habitat was designed for its future occupants at about 50 kPa, but the rover doesn't need that. Every enclosure leaks, and every airlock cycle loses a chamberful of gas, and all of it comes out of finite cylinders until the greenhouse can replace it. So the rover pressurises small enclosures, each to the least its machines need, and the numbers come straight from the formulas:

  • Liquid water needs more than water's vapour pressure: 0.87 kPa at 5 °C, 2.34 kPa at 20 °C.
  • A lift pump raising water h metres needs ρ·g·h plus that vapour pressure. On Mars a 3 m lift at 20 °C needs 13.5 kPa; on Earth the same lift would need 31.8 kPa.
  • A boiler boils where the vapour pressure reaches the enclosure's pressure: 81 °C at 50 kPa, 52 °C at 13.5 kPa.
  • Fire needs enough oxygen above the limiting fraction #40 defines, which in a thin enclosure means an oxygen-rich mix, and that raises the risk of fire.
  • Heron's automata mostly need none of this. They run on weight, sand and rope, which is why they are the rover's first companions.

The player chooses each enclosure's pressure and pays for it in gas. An enclosure run near its minimum keeps Mars visible even indoors: water boils at a low temperature and pumps come close to their limit.

This generalises the scene ambient added in #21: today the whole scene is one zone. What is genuinely new is small:

  1. The enclosure: a volume, walls with an insulation value (heat loss by Newton's law), and a leak area (gas escaping through a hole, as steam through the safety valve).
  2. A gas mixture: O₂, N₂, CO₂, H₂O and Ar tracked separately, with partial pressures adding up (Dalton's law). Sealed air today is a single gas.
  3. Oxygen-limited fire: the hearth already knows each fuel's air-fuel ratio, so it can consume O₂, make CO₂ and water vapour, and go out when too little O₂ remains.
  4. Doors and airlocks: parts that move gas between two zones when opened.

A sealed room with a fire becomes a demonstration on its own: the fire warms the air (the pressure rises, as in Heron's temple doors), uses up its oxygen, and puts itself out.

The community

The rover's first companions aren't workers. They are automata, built the way Heron built them: to have someone moving about. Heron's Automata and Pneumatica are a catalogue of machines whose elaborateness is the point: doors that open when a fire is lit, birds that sing when a vessel fills, a shrine that rolls out, performs and rolls home. For an efficient machine that complexity would be waste. For a lonely rover a long chain that rings a bell at dawn, waters the greenhouse and nudges the rover awake is a companion with a daily routine. The chain-reaction building is what the player does; loneliness is the reason to do it.

Later companions have jobs: the one that drills ice, the one that tends the greenhouse, the one that watches the airlock. A companion's character is its physics. The ice driller is patient because melting is slow, the windmill is restless and weak, and the greenhouse is fragile because a leak can kill it. The rover's loneliness has a mechanical answer: each companion is a machine it understood well enough to build.

Building stays in the existing build-mode editor, and every machine can still be written, and checked, as a #lang heroic Racket program.

Missing components

Seven parts, in order of how many automata each one unlocks. Each is a formula, like everything else in the engine.

  1. Sound (#47). Unlocks the birds, the trumpet, the dragon, the organ and the shrine's drums and thunder. Deliberately simple: a stock tone per sound, pitched by the part's size and the zone's speed of sound (c/4L for a pipe; everything sounds 30% lower outdoors on Mars), made quieter by 10·log₁₀(ρ/ρair) dB (about 19 dB outdoors on Mars; silent in vacuum), and shown in the scene as well as heard.
  2. A valve worked by a lever or rope (#46). Unlocks the coin dispenser and the shrine's pouring milk and wine. Today a sluice's opening and a float-valve are driven by water level; this links an opening to a lever angle or rope tension. It is the simplest of the seven.
  3. A peg wheel (cam) and a ratchet (#48, #49). Unlocks the hammering shipwrights, the organ's pump drive and the self-trimming lamp. The Newcomen engine's tappets already switch its valves, but only inside the cylinder model, not as a part.
  4. A granular-flow timer (#50). Sand or millet draining through a hole is the clock of Automata. Unlike water, grain drains at a steady rate whatever its depth (Beverloo's law: flow ∝ ρ√g·(D − kd)5/2), which is why Heron used it. It is the Mars-friendly clock too: a water clock freezes or boils outdoors, while regolith drains anywhere.
  5. A moving frame: a cart (#25). Wheels whose axle rides on a free body, so the mobile shrine can roll. It overlaps with the rover-as-vehicle question (#34).
  6. A belt (#51): an endless loop between two drums, for scrolling scenery. A rope that never pushes can't do it alone.
  7. Loose balls (#52), for thunder and for the ball-and-track machines that Rube Goldberg chains like. Today free bodies are blocks.

Beyond the physics, the automata need decorative figures on their levers and axles (Dionysus, the Bacchantes, the birds). That is meshes and content, not simulation. All are filed in stage 2, Mechanisms (the cart is #25).

Salvage

The cargo lies under a dust slide. Getting it out is the first long task, and it uses machines the engine already has or has planned: terrain and water over open ground (#37), digging by the soil's shear strength (#44), fracture (#43), and the trebuchet's sling for flinging spoil and stones. Three pieces are new, filed in stage 3, Ground and water:

  • Sediment transport (#53). Flowing water scours the ground where its shear passes the grains' threshold (the Shields number) and drops it where it slows, so a released reservoir cuts a gully, as in Roman hushing. A sluice box sorts dense metal from light sand. On Mars there's a catch: outdoors a water jet boils and freezes before it can do any work, so hushing needs a pressurised dig tent or heated water. Wind moves sand outdoors, but only in winds five to ten times stronger than on Earth, and that is how the storm buried the crates.
  • Buried bodies and slides (#54). A crate under the terrain is held by the ground above it and freed once dug or scoured to within a fraction of its height. A slope steeper than its angle of repose slides and buries what lies below.
  • Grip and carry (#55). Hooks, tongs, claws and the rover's arm that pick up a loose body and let it go on cue, so a crane can lift a crate and a machine can load a trebuchet. On Mars the same tongs hold 2.6 times the mass.

Could it rain?

Not from anything the rover can do, but it can make rain indoors. For rain to reach the ground, the air must be warm enough for liquid drops and dense enough that they don't boil on the way down. Mars fails both. Its mean surface pressure (610 Pa) is almost exactly water's triple point, so liquid water can barely exist, and its mean temperature is −63 °C. It does snow: Phoenix watched water-ice snow fall from clouds in 2008 and evaporate before landing, and CO₂ snow falls on the poles in winter.

Real terraforming would mean warming the planet by tens of kelvin and thickening the air many times. Even releasing all the CO₂ in the polar caps and soil would reach only about 2 kPa (Jakosky and Edwards, 2018). Proposals rely on levers such as super-greenhouse gases or engineered particles, not on energy supplied directly. For scale, the sunlight Mars intercepts is about 2 × 10¹⁶ W. The rover's machines make hundreds of watts, about 10⁻¹⁴ of that.

A sealed, warm enclosure can rain, though. This is paraterraforming, a "world-house": a pond warmed by mirrors evaporates, the vapour condenses on a roof chilled by Mars outside, and drops fall back. The rain rate is the heat lost through the roof divided by water's latent heat: 1 kW through the roof condenses about 1.6 kg of water an hour. It is a small closed weather system, a heat engine the player can watch.

Glass and the rain-house

Rain is a poor engine but a good pump. The fall returns only g·h of the 2.26 MJ each kilogram took to evaporate: a 5 m roof on Mars keeps 19 J/kg, about 0.0008%. Caught in a gutter at the roof, though, the rain fills a high reservoir with no moving parts, and a sluice releases it when a machine needs it. With 5 kW of mirror heat:

  • One sol lifts about 200 kg by 5 m: 3.7 kJ, about 1 Wh. Released over a minute that is about 60 W, a burst big enough to open the temple doors or run a fountain.
  • One month stores about 6 t. For mining, water mass matters more than its energy: six tonnes released at once is a flood that scours a slope (#53), Roman hushing on a timer.

Enclosures that need light need glass. The cargo's habitat membranes are opaque layered fabric, not film, and the storm shredded most of them: what survives makes a few small tents for water and fire, never a window (#57, #66). So glass, made from sand and sunlight, is the only way to let light into a greenhouse, a rain-house or a solar-heated vault. It is optional on the fastest route and the natural upgrade after it. Three limits shape it:

  • Temperature, not just energy. A target under concentrated sunlight heats until it radiates away what it receives, εσT⁴ = C·I. Ten flat heliostats (C ≈ 10) peak near 270 °C; melting basalt near 1,200 °C needs C around 1,000, a curved burning mirror or a lens. Melting takes about 1.8 MJ/kg, so a 10 kg pane takes an hour of a 5 kW focused beam.
  • The sand. Most Mars sand is basalt, which melts to dark, nearly opaque glass. Clear glass needs silica-rich sand, like the nearly pure silica Spirit found at Home Plate, which gives the rover a reason to explore.
  • Pressure. A pane's bending stress goes as (size/thickness)². At 13.5 kPa a 1 m pane must be 2.4 cm thick, a 30 cm pane 7 mm, so walls are small panes in a frame, as Roman windows were.

Filed in stage 4, Heat and air: glassmaking (#56), glass walls (#57), and evaporation and condensation (#58).

Trees, oxygen and wood

The cargo's seed stock includes a few tree species, and a glazed greenhouse can grow them (#42). The fastest woody plants (willow and poplar grown for coppicing, and bamboo, a woody grass) make 1–2 kg of dry wood per square metre per year on Earth. Mars sunlight is 43% of Earth's and glass takes a little more, so expect about half a kilogram per square metre per year unless mirrors add light: roughly 5 kg a year from a 10 m² greenhouse.

Oxygen: yes, measurably. Photosynthesis releases about 1.07 kg of O₂ per kg of dry wood grown (CO₂ + H₂O → CH₂O + O₂), so 5 kg a year is about 15 g of O₂ a day. A 30 m³ greenhouse at 13.5 kPa holds only about 1.1 kg of O₂, so its oxygen rises about 1.4% a day and doubles in under three months, if the rover pumps in Mars's CO₂ to feed the plants. The rover doesn't breathe, so this oxygen is for fire.

Burning: yes, but it gives back exactly what growing took. Burning the wood uses the same oxygen the tree made, about 1.2 kg per kg, and returns the CO₂. Grow-then-burn is a closed loop, with no oxygen profit. A kilogram of dry wood gives about 18 MJ of heat, so a year's 5 kg is about 90 MJ: a 50 W fire for three weeks, or about 1.25 kWh of electricity through a 5% steam engine. As a power source wood is poor, since the tree kept about 1% of the light that fell on it and the mirrors kept far more.

Splitting water for oxygen is allowed and modelled (#42), but it is usually a poor trade. Electrolysis costs at least 17.9 MJ per kg of O₂, so burning a kilogram of wood with electrolysed oxygen spends about 21 MJ of electricity to get 18 MJ of heat. It can still be the right move sometimes: turning surplus windmill power into stored oxygen before a storm, lighting a fire when the greenhouse is young, or when the hydrogen is wanted too. The player finds out by measuring.

Wood's real value is storage that outlasts the sun. A dust storm is weeks without sunlight, which is what killed Opportunity. Mirrors stop, the rain-house stops, but a woodpile and a tank of oxygen still burn. Stockpiling wood, oxygen and high water before the next storm is the game's natural late challenge, and wood is also the rover's best building timber.

Time and waiting

These loops are slow because the physics is slow, and that is honest: a reservoir fills over a month, a tree grows over years. Some approximate timescales:

Four things keep that from being dull waiting. First, sleep (#59): real rovers sleep through the night to save power, so this rover can sleep until a condition is met ("wake when the reservoir is full", "wake if the greenhouse fire goes out"), and the sim runs ahead as fast as it can at its normal step. A sleep under two hours keeps the machines turning, so its result is the same as watching; a sleep of over two hours pauses the physics engine by default (the sleep panel's Keep machines turning box, ticked, runs them for real, more slowly, and the rover's hint says so), so geared trains and the rotors on them stop; there a generator on such a rotor charges its bank at its last steady rate, the mean of its last ten seconds of charging once each second is within 5% of it, by the bank's own rules (nothing while full or outside 0 to 45 °C, the charge filed under the prime mover). That is an approximation, and the sleep panel says so: the wind's changes over the night (map wind, veer) are not followed. A generator that has never charged ten steady seconds (its bank was cold) charges at an estimate instead, marked so: the operating point where the sails' torque in the present wind meets the generator's, seen through the train's ratio and losses, the same torque balance the sim steps to (found-electrics: 278.30 W estimated, 278.29 W charged, 278.55 W watched). A generator in a crate machine turned over a world shaft from the player's windmill counts that windmill as its prime mover, by name and speed. A sleep that keeps the machines turning (#207) is exactly watching, at the engine's pace (about 5 sim-seconds a second); a generator the sim turns itself, such as a Stirling engine's, keeps its real power either way. It wakes early for anything the player marked as important. Every quantity is a formula, so the time to a target can often be predicted and shown before the player commits. Second, parallel loops: while the rain-house fills, the player is digging, melting glass or building an automaton, so waiting on one loop is working on another. Third, scale is a choice: twice the mirror area fills the reservoir twice as fast, so the player trades glass and effort against time. Fourth, tuning, below.

Game tuning

Real Mars is stingy and slow, and a game can be kinder without lying. The rule: change the numbers, never the formulas (#60). Every tweak, basic or advanced, is a number fed into an unchanged formula, so the predictions and tests stay valid: a tuned scenario states its numbers and the formula still gives the traced result.

  • Generous geography. Put a silica-rich sand deposit near the landing site, a shallow ice table, a sheltered slope for the dig, a richer seed stock. Real Mars has all of these somewhere; the scenario just puts them close. This needs no label.
  • Labelled rate multipliers. Named per-process factors, such as plant growth ×20 (trees in weeks, not years), basalt glass as clear as silica, faster evaporation, slower electronics wear. Each scales one input to one formula, as Minecraft speeds up its crops. The scenario panel lists every active one ("Plant growth ×20: real trees take years"), numbers they affect are marked in the HUD, and a Real Mars preset sets them all to 1.
  • Advanced tuning: gravity, the gas laws' constants, latent heats, efficiencies and temperature limits can also be changed, under a separate Advanced panel with a clear warning. These carry the game's lessons (rain is a poor engine, flat mirrors can't melt basalt, wood gives back only what the tree took), so changing them changes what the player learns. They are there for experiments ("what if Mars had Earth's gravity?") and for tuning difficulty, not for everyday play.

Open questions

  • Should the middle be required? Settled: no. The fastest route skips glass and stays interesting; glass is the natural upgrade because membranes are opaque, and large enclosures need it because few membranes survived (see Glass).
  • Game or sandbox? Settled: a game, won by calling Earth (see The goal). Still open: what does the ending show: the first people arriving to what the rover built?
  • How literal is Mars? Real seasons and dust storms change pressure by ±30% and sunlight by far more. Do they belong in the first version?
  • Electricity. Settled: found parts and unlimited wire, with no circuit model (#64), so the game doesn't wait on #6's electrical network.
  • How fast does electronics wear? Wear drives the economy. Too slow and the finite column never bites; too fast and the game becomes a scramble. Real panel dust loss (Opportunity lost a few tenths of a percent of power per sol between cleaning events) is one calibration point.
  • Are the missing components milestones? Settled: the mechanisms are stage 2, built and playable on Earth before any Mars work (see Build order).
  • Rover as player. Settled (2026-10-08): the player is the rover. The rover is a body in the world that the player drives, with a follow camera (#94), and in the game every action passes the rover's capability check (#163): the player can do only what the rover could do. Machine test runs outside the game still let a person operate any control and drag any object.
